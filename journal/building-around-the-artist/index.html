Building Around the Artist

The Indian indie ecosystem — who does what, how to set up, and how to choose partners without giving away the work.

Nishant Bhaskar · 28 Aug 2026

Opening Value web The manager Two systems The ladder Runway Ownership Rights & admin Three models Partners Deal hygiene Money Core team

This manual is for a musician who is past the beginning. The songs exist. Maybe a few DIY releases are already out. The question now is not only how do I grow? It is what needs to exist around me so I can keep making good work, reach people, earn, and still recognise myself in the career I am building?

An independent artist increasingly operates inside a small creative enterprise: collaborators, rights, releases, money, visuals, live, marketing and administration all have to be coordinated somehow. But the artist should not have to become every department. This manual maps the terrain around the artist — who does what, what should remain close to the artist, what can be delegated, and how to structure the system without casually giving away the work. Its companion, Getting the Work to People, covers the operating rhythm — releases, discovery, growth.

Two things to hold while reading. First, the manual works from a concrete case — an English-first singer-songwriter lane, an India base, a batch of songs ready — because specific numbers are more useful than safe generalities. If your lane is electronic or Hindi-first, the structures hold; recalibrate the numbers. Second, fees, revenue shares and rosters shift constantly; time-sensitive figures are dated in their relevant sections.

You do not need all of this at once. Most artists build the surrounding system gradually, as new bottlenecks appear. Use the manual to see what may eventually matter — not as a checklist of things that must exist before you can begin.

Much of what follows is about sequencing decisions in a way that keeps your options open later. The sequence itself is not sacred: different artists have different clocks, different levels of runway and very different ways of meeting an audience. The job is to build the right system around the artist, not fit the artist into the system.

01 · The system

The value web

Five layers around the artist — from creation and artist world to discovery and the places where relationships and value compound.

Almost everyone you will work with sits in one of five layers. Knowing which layer someone belongs to tells you what they can do for you — and what they cannot.

Layer 1 · Creation core

The people who directly touch the music

Songwriter and composer (the source IP), producers, co-writers and lyricists, session musicians, mix and master engineers, vocal coaches and arrangers.

Value created: songs, craft, sound, identity  ·  Risk if mismanaged: ownership disputes, sonic inconsistency
Layer 2 · Artist world

The people who help the work become a world

Creative director, photographer, filmmaker, visual artist, graphic designer, stylist, stage and A/V designer — and, where relevant, the people shaping physical releases, objects and spaces around the work.

Value created: coherence, visual memory, emotional depth, a recognisable world  ·  Risk if mismanaged: generic branding, fragmented aesthetic, promotion that feels detached from the artist
Layer 3 · Operations & infrastructure

The plumbing

Manager, lawyer, accountant, publishing administrator, label-services partner (distribution plus campaign support — section 07), metadata and catalogue admin. The best version of this layer removes friction and protects creative attention; the worst version becomes the artist's full-time job.

Value created: speed, protection, clarity, scalability  ·  Risk if mismanaged: chaos, leakage, bad deals, delayed releases
Layer 4 · Discovery & amplification

How the world finds you

DSP editors and recommendation systems (DSPs — digital service providers: Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube Music and the rest), playlist curators, PR and publicists, radio, content and creator partners, trusted media, tastemakers and other discovery surfaces. Distribution gets the music into the market; this layer is about whether the right people encounter it and care.

Value created: discovery, narrative, audience signals, repeat attention  ·  Risk if mismanaged: great music with no audience — or attention that never becomes affinity
Layer 5 · Live, cultural & commercial surface

Where relationships and value compound

Booking agents, festival programmers, venue promoters, tour managers, fan communities, brand managers, sync agents, publishers for placements, collaborating artists and scene-builders.

Value created: fandom, reputation, revenue, relationships, career resilience  ·  Risk if mismanaged: burnout, mis-positioning, short-termism
Two systems around the artist

The web becomes easier to read if you separate two systems. The creative system makes the work and the artist better: producers, writers, musicians, peers, mentors and visual collaborators. The career system coordinates the work around the work: manager, lawyer, label-services, booking, PR, rights, finance and administration.

The artist sits at the centre of both. The manager may coordinate much of the career system, but should not automatically become the creative centre.

02 · The system

The manager

Career architect, operator, buffer, negotiator — often at the centre of the career system.

A good manager sets release strategy, coordinates label-services, PR, booking and commercial partners, negotiates deals, protects the artist's time and energy, filters opportunities and keeps the long game visible. The tell of a real one: they think 12–24 months ahead, not 12–24 days — and they know which decisions belong to the artist rather than to the operation.

Part CEO, part chief of staff, part therapist, part lawyer-in-training, part politician. The aim is not to shield the artist from reality. It is to stop coordination from consuming the attention required to make the work.

What a manager is not

Not just a booking agent. Not a calendar manager. Not a WhatsApp responder. If that is all they are doing, you have hired an assistant and are paying for a manager.

03 · The system

How the two systems work together

The artist remains the centre; different people carry different kinds of responsibility.

Careers do not move through one clean hierarchy. A producer or collaborator can change the work more profoundly than a campaign; a venue or scene can change who encounters it; a manager can change the quality of every decision around it. The useful distinction is not "who matters most?" but "what kind of value is this person responsible for?"

A strong manager connects the two systems without turning every creative choice into an operational one.

The operating question

What must remain close to the artist because it requires taste, intent or conviction? What can the surrounding team absorb because it is coordination, administration or repeatable execution? Get that boundary wrong and either the artist loses control — or the artist becomes the entire back office.

The dashboard is not the career

Above 20K, partners tend to stop asking about the numbers first. Below it they will still take the meeting — you'll just be arguing from the music rather than the dashboard. The 2–20K band includes some of the most respected names in the scene, and monthly listeners swing week to week: read the bands, not the digits.

They also do not tell you whether people care. Two artists with the same listener count can have very different careers if one has repeat listeners, a room they can fill and people who will travel or pay to see them. Read reach alongside retention, live draw and fan behaviour.

Interlude · The artist

The artist's runway

Talent is not the only thing that determines how long someone gets to keep trying.

Two equally talented artists can face completely different career choices. One may have family support, savings, housing, equipment, connections and the freedom to spend a year making a record or losing money on small international shows. Another may need a job, session work, weddings, teaching or commercial voice work simply to keep the creative project alive.

That does not make one artist more serious than the other. It means persistence itself has a cost.

RunwayWhat it gives the artist
Financial capitalTime, travel, production, the ability to absorb failed bets and decline bad deals
Social capitalIntroductions, collaborators, managers, venues, information and faster access to opportunity
Creative capitalCraft, repertoire, identity, catalogue, stage ability and trusted collaborators
Emotional capitalPeople to absorb disappointment, celebrate progress and make the career less solitary
Money changes the choice set

Money is not only a production budget. It buys time and optionality: the ability to wait, tour small rooms, hire the right collaborator, make the visual properly, move cities, decline a bad deal, or keep going through a period when the work is not yet economically obvious. Any serious operating plan should answer a basic question early: what finances the runway?

Part two

Setting up

Ownership, registrations, operating models, partners, deals. The unglamorous half — and the half that decides who owns the work in ten years.

05 · The setup

Ownership as the north star

Treat the project as a creative enterprise — and know exactly what it owns.

The principle is not "own everything at any cost." It is know what you own, know why someone else owns a share, and do not trade permanent rights for short-term services by accident. Keep ownership explicit, documented and proportionate to the real creative and financial contribution. Four principles carry it:

  • Protect the IP. Default to licensing rather than permanently assigning rights where that is commercially possible. Treat any permanent transfer as a deliberate trade for clear upside, not as the standard price of access.
  • Operate like a modern label. Build a small senior core around the artist, then plug into distribution, PR, booking, rights and commercial specialists when needed. The artist should remain close to intent and major choices; the team should absorb coordination.
  • Design for scalability. Every creative and business decision should flex across mediums: studio, stage, screen and physical world.
  • Measure momentum. Every release is a data point. Track, learn, adjust.

The entity

Separate the project's money early, even before a formal company is necessary — dedicated records for project expenses, royalties, collaborations and income. Mixing personal and project money is the mess your accountant will spend the most hours untangling.

Incorporation becomes useful when contracting, liability, regular invoicing, collaborators, tax or longer-term ownership structures justify the compliance. The usual Indian options are a One Person Company, an LLP, or a private limited company — a Pvt Ltd needs two directors, which many solo artists solve with a family member or trusted friend. Choose with a CA based on the actual project, not because a "serious artist" is supposed to incorporate. Budget ₹10–20K to incorporate plus recurring compliance each year, and register for GST before you invoice your first brand or overseas partner.

Worked example — what "goals" look like when they mean something

By month 12: a defined 30–45 minute exportable live set · clean rights, splits and metadata across the catalogue · one coordinating point in place.

By month 18: 8–10 paid live appearances · global representation secured in at least one of booking, sync or publishing — while retaining full IP ownership.

Notice what these have in common: each is verifiable, dated, and something the artist controls. "Get bigger" is not a goal. The audience-side targets — listeners, save rate, press, editorial — live in the Engine's measurement section, where they can sit beside the metrics that qualify them.

And they are one case's choices. Set your own against the ladder in section 04, not against this example — the ladder shows respected artists a decade in sitting below some of these targets.

06 · The setup

Rights & admin

The paperwork that determines who gets paid, for as long as the songs earn.

Before anything else, confirm your existing catalogue is clean: every collaborator on released material compensated, no external ownership claims, masters fully yours. If any of that is unclear, resolve it before adding new partners.

Rights clarity is not the opposite of collaboration. Genuine co-writing and creative contribution can create legitimate ownership; the point is to agree it while the work is being made rather than reconstruct it after the song succeeds.

Then, for everything new:

Boring, and the reason you can answer a sync request in hours instead of a week — which is often the whole competition. Clean admin lets the creative team move faster when an opportunity appears.

07 · The setup

Three ways to run the project

Three operating modes. Artists may move between them, combine them, or stay in one longer than expected.

Model A · Self-run

Direct ownership

You handle operations yourself: registrations, metadata, freelance PR, DSP uploads.

Good for: ultra-early stage, testing sonic direction, building foundational assets. Pressure point: when coordination starts eating the time and attention needed for the music.
Model B · Boutique partnership

Manager or agency-led

A full-service boutique team runs strategy, rollout, PR coordination, DSP pitching and showcase submissions — you keep full IP.

Typical cost: ₹50K–1.5L/month retainer plus 15–20% commission — if both, ask for the retainer to be recoupable against commission. Good for: artists with enough runway or revenue to buy senior judgment and coordination without surrendering ownership.
Model C · Label-services

Professional distribution + campaign support

A label-services company handles distribution, playlist pitching, analytics, marketing, sync and brand tie-ups. You retain all rights.

Typical cost: revenue share (you keep 80–90%) or project fees of ₹1–3L per release cycle. Good for: artists with enough momentum, catalogue and economics to justify heavier distribution and campaign infrastructure.

Choose the model based on the artist's stage, runway, desired involvement and the actual bottleneck. If the problem is taste or artistic direction, adding more operational infrastructure will not solve it.

08 · The setup

Partners — who, for what, and when

A starting map, by career stage. Approach the right kind of partner at the right moment.

One note before the tables: several of these partners are also labels. That isn't disqualifying — but it means a deal may involve your masters, so the section 09 clauses apply to them hardest. Ask in the first meeting whether it does. (Partner descriptions last checked August 2026.)

Organisations in this space cluster into a few recurring kinds: career architects who build long-term positioning for a small roster; development studios that make records and moments; scaled platforms that move shows, tours and festivals; cultural engines that build scenes and showcases; commercial partners who convert audience into brand income; and PR firms handling narrative and visibility. Most do more than one. Knowing which job you actually need — and which an organisation is genuinely strongest at — prevents the most common mismatch in the scene.

The organisations below are reference points, not a complete map of the ecosystem. There are many smaller managers, boutique agencies, independent operators and specialists who may be a better fit depending on the artist's stage, ambition and immediate need. Use the kinds above to size up anyone not listed here: ask what they are strongest at, what they do themselves, how much attention the artist will receive, how they make money, and whether the working style fits.

Finding the right fit

Do not choose partners only from the most visible names. The music ecosystem is much larger than any public directory, and many strong collaborators are discovered through conferences, showcases, one-to-one meetings, mentorship sessions, referrals and conversations with other people in the industry.

Do the homework. Understand the job you actually need done, speak to multiple people, check how they work, and choose for fit with the artist's stage, ambition, temperament and actual need — not for logo value.

The goal is not to assemble the most impressive team. It is to assemble the right circle for the artist at this stage.

One possible three-year pathway

This is an example of sequencing, not a maturity law. Some artists stay self-run longer, some find management early, some build through live or scenes before any label-services relationship makes sense.

Avoid overlapping mandates: one clear coordinating point, one distribution route.

Year 1 — establish one clear coordinating point, clean distribution and basic operating discipline.

Year 2 — add specialist PR, sync, booking or other support where a real bottleneck emerges.

Year 3+ — add international representation or higher-touch infrastructure when the audience and economics justify it.

09 · The setup

Deal hygiene

Six clauses that decide how a partnership ends.

Every partner agreement — label-services, boutique, distribution — should respect these principles:

  • Default to licensing before assignment. A permanent transfer of masters should require exceptional, explicit upside. Where rights are licensed, define term, territory and reversion clearly: rights return after 3–5 years, or earlier if performance targets aren't met.
  • Fixed terms only. No vague "options" on future albums or EPs. If a partner wants the next project, they can earn it project by project.
  • Release commitments in writing. The partner commits to timelines and minimum marketing deliverables — spend, asset count, playlist-pitch obligations.
  • Define approvals deliberately. Protect approval or meaningful consultation rights over the decisions that can materially change the work or the artist's identity: masters, artwork, key collaborators, brand associations and sensitive sync uses.
  • Territory carve-backs. Start India plus global digital. If a partner passes on a region or format, carve it back out and pursue it elsewhere.
  • Statements and audit rights. Royalty statements at least quarterly, with explicit audit rights for you or your accountant.

Featured collaborators are a different document: performance share, credit wording, approval over reuse, and any writing share — settled on a split sheet before the session, not after the song works.

Credit is part of the deal too. Properly recognising genuine contributors is not only fair; it creates a wider circle of people invested in the work and more likely to advocate for it.

And one habit beside the clauses: keep data access transparent and real-time throughout.

The visual budget is the easiest to defer and the hardest to retrofit — you cannot go back and re-shoot a campaign that already ran. Layer 2 of the value web is where recall lives; underfunding it produces exactly the failure that layer warned about — a fragmented aesthetic, a forgettable brand.

The harder point is not the exact number. It is that the ability to carry this cost changes the artist's choices. The career clock is partly financial.

In this worked example, a full year of the operating stack can reach roughly ₹15–35 lakh, and streaming at the listener levels in this manual is unlikely to finance that by itself. The runway may come from live fees, sync, session work, teaching, voice work, savings, family support, employment, advances or outside backing. Decide the financing mix before choosing the cadence. A release plan that assumes money will somehow appear is not a plan.

The question the runway interlude asked comes due here: who finances the years before the artist becomes economically obvious?

11 · The team

The core team

As the career grows: a small operating core, surrounded by a wider creative circle. This is not a day-one hiring plan.

  • Manager / senior operator — timelines, partner coordination, negotiations and long-range planning. Often the first senior operating hire once coordination becomes the bottleneck; not automatically the first person every artist needs.
  • Producer / mix / mastering relationships — prioritise recurring collaborators where they improve the work and create continuity. One person does not need to do all three jobs.
  • Live director (when needed) — builds the 30–45 minute exportable set.
  • Artist-world collaborators — photo, film, design, styling, stage and physical objects as a coherent system rather than disconnected campaign vendors.
  • Lawyer & accountant — registrations, splits, the audit trail.
  • The circle — bandmates, peers, producers, mentors and trusted friends who are not necessarily on payroll but make the creative life less solitary, challenge weak work, introduce collaborators and give the artist people to celebrate with.

Add people when the bottleneck becomes real.

The closing thought

Most of this manual does not grow an audience directly. The terrain work — ownership, registrations, the right partners, clean deals and a team that protects attention — is what makes growth survivable. Plenty of artists have grown fast inside bad structures. The growth was real; the upside, time or creative control went somewhere else.

Set the terrain first. Then run the engine. But remember what the engine is for: to help the artist keep making work worth carrying forward.

Time-sensitive figures, rosters and platform policies are dated in their relevant sections and will drift. The structures — the value web, the recurring partner types, the deal principles — should outlast the numbers.
