The Terrain

The Indian indie ecosystem — who does what, how to set up, and how to choose partners without giving away the work.

Opening Value web The manager How it works The agencies The ladder Ownership Rights & admin Three models Partners Deal hygiene Money Core team

This manual is for a musician who is past the beginning. The songs exist. Maybe a few DIY releases are out. The question now is not how do I make music but how do I run this seriously without signing away what I've made?

It covers the terrain: the people around an artist, what each one actually does, which agencies operate in which lane, and how to structure the deals. Its companion, The Engine, covers the operating rhythm — releases, content, growth.

Two things to hold while reading. First, the manual works from a concrete case — an English-first singer-songwriter lane, an India base, a batch of songs ready — because specific numbers are more useful than safe generalities. If your lane is electronic or Hindi-first, the structures hold; recalibrate the numbers. Second, fees, revenue shares and rosters shift constantly. Figures here are indicative as of early 2026.

Much of what follows is sequence — doing things in an order that keeps your options open later.

01 · The system

The value web

Five concentric layers around the artist — from creation to distribution to the places where value compounds.

Almost everyone you will work with sits in one of five layers. Knowing which layer someone belongs to tells you what they can do for you — and what they cannot.

Layer 1 · Creation core

The people who directly touch the music

Songwriter and composer (the source IP), producers, co-writers and lyricists, session musicians, mix and master engineers, vocal coaches and arrangers.

Value created: songs, sound, identity  ·  Risk if mismanaged: ownership disputes, sonic inconsistency
Layer 2 · Creative translation

The people who turn sound into a world

Creative director, photographer, videographer and director, motion designer, stylist, stage and A/V designer.

Value created: visual identity, recall, differentiation  ·  Risk if mismanaged: fragmented aesthetic, forgettable brand
Layer 3 · Operations & infrastructure

The plumbing

Manager, lawyer, accountant, publishing administrator, label-services partner (distribution plus campaign support — section 08), metadata and catalogue admin.

Value created: speed, protection, clarity, scalability  ·  Risk if mismanaged: chaos, leakage, bad deals, delayed releases
Layer 4 · Distribution & amplification

How the world finds you

DSPs (digital service providers — Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube Music and the rest) and their editors, playlist curators, PR and publicists, radio pluggers, content strategists, creator partners, brand partnership teams.

Value created: discovery, narrative, scale  ·  Risk if mismanaged: great music, no audience
Layer 5 · Live, cultural & commercial surface

Where value compounds

Booking agents, festival programmers, venue promoters, tour managers, brand managers, sync agents, publishers for placements, collaborating artists.

Value created: revenue, reputation, career velocity  ·  Risk if mismanaged: burnout, mis-positioning, short-termism
02 · The system

The manager

Career architect, operator, buffer, negotiator — sitting at the centre of the web.

A good manager sets release strategy, coordinates label-services, PR, booking and creatives, negotiates deals, protects your time and energy, filters opportunities, and builds long-term positioning. The tell of a real one: they think 12–24 months ahead, not 12–24 days.

Part CEO, part chief of staff, part therapist, part lawyer-in-training, part politician.

What a manager is not

Not just a booking agent. Not a calendar manager. Not a WhatsApp responder. If that is all they are doing, you have hired an assistant and are paying for a manager.

03 · The system

How the system actually works

Who actually moves a career — and in what order.

Ranked by influence on trajectory: the artist first, then the manager, then the label or services partner, then the publisher, then PR — then everyone else. Spend your attention in that order.

In motion, it looks like this: the manager orchestrates everyone. Label-services distributes and pitches. PR amplifies the narrative. The publishing admin collects composition money. The metadata admin makes sure nobody corrupts the data underneath it all.

When a piece is missing

Things still work. But they leak, stall, or stay small.

04 · The system

The agency landscape

Six species of partner. Knowing which one you're talking to prevents the most common mismatch in the scene.

Indian indie agencies get described with one word — "management" — but they do six different jobs. Approach a cultural engine expecting career architecture, or a scaled platform expecting bespoke positioning, and both sides end up frustrated.

Boutique career architects

Highly selective, long-term orientation, cross-format and export-focused, narrative-driven. Small rosters by design.

They curate. They don't spray and pray.

Development studios

Tiny roster, high-touch. Record, narrative and campaign builders — structured artist development, not just distribution.

They build moments, not full careers.

Scaled talent & infrastructure platforms

Move shows, tours, festivals and IP. Volume and repeatable formats; execution over bespoke design.

They move careers. They don't architect them.

Cultural engines

Shape scenes and taste. Build platforms, showcases and IP; create gravity and visibility.

They shape the environment you grow in, not your trajectory.

Monetisation layers

Brand deals, creator-economy representation, commercial partnerships.

They optimise income streams, not career arcs.

Amplification layers

PR, communications, campaign visibility, media positioning.

They amplify. They don't represent.

Before any meeting, place the agency in one of these six boxes. Then ask for what that box can actually deliver. Section 09 names names.

Part two

Setting up

Ownership, registrations, operating models, partners, deals. The unglamorous half — and the half that decides who owns the work in ten years.

06 · The setup

Ownership as the north star

Operate as the founder-CEO of a creative enterprise. Everything else follows from that.

The model: retain all master and publishing rights, and engage professional collaborators as paid partners rather than co-owners. Four principles carry it:

  • Own the IP. License, don't sell. Retain 100% of masters and publishing unless a deal offers clear, time-bound upside.
  • Operate like a modern label. A small senior core — manager, creative producer, designer — plugged into distribution, PR and brand specialists per project.
  • Design for scalability. Every creative and business decision should flex across mediums: studio, stage, screen.
  • Measure momentum. Every release is a data point. Track, learn, adjust.

The entity

Incorporate. The usual candidates are a One Person Company, an LLP, or a private limited company — a Pvt Ltd needs two directors, which many solo artists solve with a family member or trusted friend. Which structure fits depends on the scope of what you're building and your ambitions for it; decide with a CA, not a search engine. Budget ₹10–20K to incorporate, plus recurring compliance costs each year, and register for GST before you invoice your first brand or overseas partner. Keep separate accounts for business expenses, royalties and collaborations — mixing personal and project money is the mess your accountant will spend the most hours untangling.

Worked example — what "goals" look like when they mean something

By month 12: 25–35K monthly listeners (parity with the mid-band of the ladder above) · a 6–8% Spotify save rate — retention, not just discovery · one long-form press feature · one DSP editorial inclusion · one Indian festival invitation · a defined 30–45 minute exportable live set.

By month 18: first international editorial inclusion · first global showcase invitation · 8–10 paid live appearances · global representation secured in at least one of booking, sync or publishing — while retaining full IP ownership.

Notice what these have in common: each is verifiable, dated, and tied to a system explained in this manual or its companion. "Get bigger" is not a goal.

And they are one case's choices. Set your own numbers against the ladder in section 05, not against this example — the ladder shows respected artists a decade in sitting below some of these targets.

07 · The setup

Rights & admin

The paperwork that determines who gets paid, for as long as the songs earn.

Before anything else, confirm your existing catalogue is clean: every collaborator on released material compensated, no external ownership claims, masters fully yours. If any of that is unclear, resolve it before adding new partners.

Then, for everything new:

The metadata archive must hold: ISRCs, writer and publisher credits, lyrics, stems, instrumentals, TV-safe edits, artwork, mood and genre tags. Boring, and the reason you can answer a sync request in hours instead of a week — which is often the whole competition.

08 · The setup

Three ways to run the project

Not mutually exclusive. Most artists evolve A → B → C.

Model A · Self-run

Direct ownership

You handle operations yourself: registrations, metadata, freelance PR, DSP uploads.

Good for: ultra-early stage, testing sonic direction, building foundational assets. Stops working: once rollout frequency increases — operations eat the writing time.
Model B · Boutique partnership

Manager or agency-led

A full-service boutique team runs strategy, rollout, PR coordination, DSP pitching and showcase submissions — you keep full IP.

Typical cost: ₹50K–1.5L/month retainer plus 15–20% commission — if both, ask for the retainer to be recoupable against commission. Good for: artists with funding who want structure without surrendering ownership.
Model C · Label-services

Professional distribution + campaign support

A label-services company handles distribution, playlist pitching, analytics, marketing, sync and brand tie-ups. You retain all rights.

Typical cost: revenue share (you keep 80–90%) or project fees of ₹1–3L per release cycle. Good for: a global push with a full runway of songs and showcase targets.

The three-year pathway

The boutique partner is the control tower; label-services sits underneath it. One of each — not two managers, not two distributors.

Year 1 — foundation, clean data, early traction: a boutique architect or development studio; Believe or Symphonic underneath for high retention and analytics.

Year 2 — sync, festivals, brand collabs: continue with the boutique; layer in specialist PR and a sync agent. If the data earns an invitation, consider AWAL or Virgin — both are selective, and moving distributors mid-stream has costs, so weigh what you'd gain.

Year 3+ — major-scale rollouts: boutique plus a global counterpart (UK/US co-management); The Orchard via an approved imprint.

10 · The setup

Deal hygiene

Six clauses that decide how a partnership ends.

Every partner agreement — label-services, boutique, distribution — should respect these principles:

  • License, never assign. Masters are licensed, not sold. Every license carries a reversion clause: rights return to you after 3–5 years, or earlier if performance targets aren't met.
  • Fixed terms only. No vague "options" on future albums or EPs. If a partner wants the next project, they can earn it project by project.
  • Release commitments in writing. The partner commits to timelines and minimum marketing deliverables — spend, asset count, playlist-pitch obligations.
  • You keep approvals. Mixes, masters, artwork, collaborators, brand tie-ups, sync usage. All of it.
  • Territory carve-backs. Start India plus global digital. If a partner passes on a region or format, carve it back out and pursue it elsewhere.
  • Statements and audit rights. Royalty statements at least quarterly, with explicit audit rights for you or your accountant.

Featured collaborators are a different document: performance share, credit wording, approval over reuse, and any writing share — settled on a split sheet before the session, not after the song works.

And one habit beside the clauses: keep data access transparent and real-time throughout.

What it costs

The visual budget is the easiest to defer and the hardest to retrofit — you cannot go back and re-shoot a campaign that already ran. Layer 2 of the value web is where recall lives; underfunding it produces exactly the failure that layer warned about — a fragmented aesthetic, a forgettable brand.

And one honest total: run everything in this table for a year and it comes to roughly ₹15–35 lakh, which streaming income at the listener numbers in this manual will not cover. The plan is funded by live fees, sync, session work, savings, a job, or outside backing. Decide which before you start — running it on hope is how artists end up signing the deal this manual exists to help them avoid.

12 · The team

The core team

Lean but senior. Five roles, not fifteen.

  • Manager — timelines, DSP relations, festival submissions, negotiations. The first hire.
  • Mix/master engineer — one person across releases, for sonic continuity.
  • Live director (when needed) — builds the 30–45 minute exportable set.
  • Visual team — photo, motion, artwork, as one coherent system.
  • Lawyer & accountant — registrations, splits, the audit trail.
The closing thought

Nothing in this manual grows an audience. The terrain work — ownership, registrations, the right partner in the right lane, clean deals — is what makes growth survivable. Plenty of artists have grown fast inside bad deals. The growth was real; the upside went to whoever owned the masters.

Set the terrain first. Then run the engine.

Figures, rosters and platform policies in this manual date from late 2025 / early 2026 and will drift. The structures — the value web, the six agency species, the deal principles — should outlast the numbers.
