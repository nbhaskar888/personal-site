The Engine

Releases, content and the road out — the operating rhythm for the next eighteen months.

Opening The runway 180 days Content engine India live Global route The EPK Collaboration KPIs Hygiene

The companion manual, The Terrain, set up the structure: ownership, partners, deals. This one is the part you run every week — how a batch of finished songs becomes a twelve-month release campaign, a content rhythm, a live ladder and a six-month push outward.

To make it concrete, assume you have nine finished songs and want them released over the next twelve months. That is the worked example this manual runs on — an English-first singer-songwriter lane, an India base. If your batch is six songs or twelve, stretch or compress the runway; the shape holds.

One principle governs everything here: momentum beats volume. A release plan is a set of loops, not a list of dates — each single builds narrative continuity, and each release opens a fresh Release Radar window and a fresh round of listener signals, which is what actually moves algorithmic surfacing.

01 · The runway

Nine songs, twelve months

Three phases: momentum, story arc, showcase lift.

Say the nine songs are mixed and mastered. Here is one way the twelve months can play out.

Phase A · Momentum — Q1–Q2

Singles 1–3, spaced every 6–8 weeks

Each single generates a set of alternate assets — an acoustic version, a live-room video, a producer flip (a commissioned reinterpretation). The full six-asset breakdown is in section 03.

Objective: three shots at editorial and Release Radar, and three rounds of data on what listeners actually keep.

Phase B · Story arc — Q3

A 5–6 track EP

Combine the earlier singles with new material and one collaboration. Drop a music video 10–14 days after the EP to sustain editorial momentum and give press and festival curators a second hook.

Phase C · Showcase lift — Q4

A deluxe / live EP

Two or three live versions with supporting visuals, timed to land alongside global showcase submissions.

Why this shape works

Singles build anticipation and give each song its own discovery window. The EP consolidates legitimacy — for media, DSPs and fans, a body of work reads differently than a string of songs. The live phase extends shelf life and positions the project for performance slots. By month twelve you have a body of work with a shape — which is what press, DSPs and bookers actually assess.

Note the arithmetic: this runway releases six or seven of the nine songs. That is deliberate — hold the rest back. A second EP with nothing in reserve is how year two stalls.

The first 180 days

A micro-plan around Single #1. Day 0 is its release day — the first row runs up to it.

WindowFocusKey actions
The run-up (six weeks before Day 0)Prep & Single #1 Finalise the master; assign ISRC/UPC; complete rights registrations. Upload to the distributor four to five weeks out — the editorial pitch needs the track already delivered. Roll out the video trailer plus three micro-cuts. Announce two-city micro-shows. Start pitching label-service partners with the early data.
Days 1–90Single #2 Release the lyric or visualiser video. Film a live session in a distinctive venue. Assemble and circulate a festival-ready EPK (section 06).
Days 91–150Single #3 Drop the third video. Stage two curated listening sessions — 100–150 capacity, designed environment, full visual production — plus one access show (open mic, smaller club, college slot) for reach. Commission a producer flip. Prep EP masters, artwork and metadata.
Days 151–180EP prep Announce the EP with a Bandcamp pre-order. Release one acoustic pre-drop. Lock the design collaborator for EP art and capsule merch. Begin global showcase submissions.
The deadline that bites

Spotify's editorial pitch cutoff is seven days before release, but pitches submitted three to four weeks ahead get far more consideration — treat four weeks as your deadline, not seven days. Keep a T-30 checklist — a fixed list completed thirty days before each release day: split sheets, ISRCs, artwork, metadata, pitch submitted. (T-30 means thirty days before release; the same notation runs through this manual.) Miss it and the song ships with its main discovery mechanism never armed.

The always-on layer

Between campaigns, a weekly cadence: one writing or rehearsal snippet, one BTS or process moment, one collab or cover post, one lyric or micro-story reel. Keep a rolling content calendar tied to each release's T-timeline. Plan the campaign assets and the weekly layer ahead — nothing should be improvised the week it's posted.

04 · The world

The live ladder — India first

Months 2–10. Calibrated to a singer-songwriter / emotional-pop lane; re-rank for other lanes.

Ziro Festival of Music
Curated discovery lineup; the performance-plus-storytelling format suits song-led sets. A natural first target.
NH7 Weekender
India's best-known indie festival brand, with real press exposure — but it books established acts and has skipped years recently. A later-stage target; confirm the edition is happening before planning around it.
Sofar Sounds India
Intimate, vetted listening rooms in cities across India. Small fees at best — take it for the room and the footage, which becomes EPK material.
Bandland (Bangalore)
Skews rock and alternative — relevant if your live set carries real band weight; the wrong room for a quiet set.
Magnetic Fields
Only viable with placement on a singer-songwriter or acoustic stage — the festival skews electronic, so curation matters.

Showcases are signal events, not finish lines

The real value is the professional relationships and media credibility they unlock. Each one should feed back into stronger releases, better partnerships and more visibility. If a showcase produces no follow-up conversations, treat that as information for the next application — not just a bad night.

06 · The world

The EPK

One page plus a shared folder. Update after every major show or release.

  • A 150-word global bio
  • Two short press quotes
  • Hi-res photos — portrait, live, landscape — plus logo or wordmark
  • Two live performance videos with properly recorded audio — a desk feed or dedicated recording, not a phone from the back — and one music video
  • Stage plot, input list, tech rider
  • A data sheet: top cities, save rates, sold-out shows, notable collaborations
  • A contact list: manager, booking, press, label-services point

Keep one master EPK folder and never fork it. A festival programmer opening a stale kit with last year's numbers reads it as a signal about the operation, not just the music. Keep it current.

07 · Collaboration

Collaboration, in priority order

Visual and production partners first — they shape the universe. Sonic features second — only when they extend it.

  • Visual, costume, stylist. Treat as a creative co-author, not a vendor. One designer-collaborator per release cycle — artwork, video, stage object, or capsule merch (a small, limited merchandise drop).
  • Production and co-writing. One sonic collaborator per cycle who deepens the production world: a producer, instrumentalist or arranger. Not necessarily a featured vocalist.
  • Producer flips. Commission a reinterpretation — electronic or alt-pop — for live and festival use. It extends shelf life without diluting the core.
  • Sonic features, selectively. A vocalist or producer feature only when it clearly extends the universe. Cross-promotion alone is not a reason.
  • Scene cross-pollination. One collaboration per cycle with a peer act in Mumbai, Delhi or Bangalore, for audience reach. Real, but the lowest rung of this ladder.
Why this order

Features are the most visible collaboration and usually the least durable. A visual co-author changes how every future release looks; a feature changes one song's reach for one cycle. Spend the scarce slots on the compounding kind.

09 · Process

Process hygiene

Three habits that make the whole engine compound.

  • A release control sheet. One document with dates, assets, budgets and statuses. If it isn't on the sheet, it isn't happening.
  • A post-mortem after every drop. What connected, what didn't, what to replicate next cycle. Twenty minutes, written down, before the next campaign starts.
  • Sprints inside a long game. Each release is a sprint; the career is not. Build the cycle you can still run in year three — that is the actual test of a plan.
The closing thought

The difference between the artists who convert a batch of songs into a career and the ones who don't is rarely information — it's whether the loop actually runs: release, learn, adjust, release.

Application windows move every year — re-check every date in section 05 before planning around it. The loop itself — momentum phases, the T-30 checklist, post-mortems — should outlast the specifics.
